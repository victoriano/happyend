Is feel good TV over?

People say ’90s TV left you with a warm glow and that everything is dark now. We scored the 1,384 most-voted US TV series and the 2,343 most-watched US movies since 1980. Movies have barely changed. TV has changed almost completely.

Chapter 1

What is feel good?

A happy ending isn’t enough. A show or a movie is feel good if its characters end up at peace, in harmony and loved — by their family, their friends, their community, their country or the universe — and if it leaves us with the sense that there’s hope for humanity.

The verdict

Movies are as feel good as they were in the ’90s. TV isn’t.

What the data supports: the most-voted US shows have gone from an average feel good score of (premiered in the ’90s) to (2020-2025), and the share of clearly feel good shows has fallen from to . The decline shows up in almost every genre and survives every check we ran. Movies, by contrast, barely move until 2020 and dip only slightly after that.

What it suggests: part of it, roughly a third, comes from the fact that we now watch fewer animated shows and comedies and more action and crime; the rest happens within each genre (details in the appendix). Today’s shows also depict worse societies. And IMDb voters rate the most feel good titles slightly lower.

What it can’t tell us: for most shows we only know the premise, not how they end; today’s votes favor the older shows we still remember; and the scores come from language models, with no human review. Nor does it say anything about causes (prestige drama, streaming, audience tastes) or whether people miss those shows: that survey has been designed, but not run.

Appendix

For those who want more detail

What holds the story up, plus a few side findings: whether the TV decline is real or an artifact of the method, how much of it comes from watching different genres, which kinds of shows fell the most, how it fits with happy endings, what IMDb voters reward and how we measured it all.

Appendix A · Robustness checks

Is the gap real?

We read every popular show, so there’s no sampling error. But each decade is just one batch of titles, and a similar batch would have come out somewhat differently. On top of that, the scores come from language models, and most TV summaries describe the premise, not the ending. So we checked it in many different ways.

Appendix D · Endings

Ending well isn’t the same as leaving you feeling good

We also classified how each title ends: Happy · Bittersweet · Ambiguous · Tragic. For TV, that’s only possible when the summary describes the ending or the show’s pattern, which happens for a minority of shows. Where it is possible, the two measures go hand in hand, but they aren’t the same thing: some bittersweet endings are deeply feel good, and some happy endings offer no comfort at all.

Appendix E · IMDb

IMDb voters don’t reward feel good

We can’t know whether people miss ’90s TV; that would take a survey. But among movies from the same year and genre, each extra point of feel good is associated with points of IMDb rating. Today’s most-voted shows, like Breaking Bad (9.5 on IMDb and a 1 for feel good), point the same way.

Appendix F · How we did it

Two artificial readers and a referee

Nobody can watch 1,384 TV series in an afternoon, but you can read their summaries. Here’s how it worked:

Catalog. Fiction TV series and movies with IMDb votes, made in the US (country of origin per TMDB and Wikidata; mostly-foreign co-productions excluded).
Census. The 30 most-voted shows of every premiere year and the 50 most-voted movies of every year: all of them, no sampling.
Summaries. Wikipedia (September 2026 dump) in whichever language was available; if there was none, the TMDB synopsis. Titles and years scrubbed.
Two annotators. Two different language models scored each summary independently, following a rulebook, and explained their score.
Referee. A third model settled disagreements (about the ending, the audience, and whenever scores were 3 or more points apart) without knowing who had said what.

The two annotators agreed a lot: for TV, their feel good scores were within 2 points of each other in of cases (Krippendorff’s α ); for movies, α . But that doesn’t mean they’re always right: two machines can be wrong in the same way.

What you should know before you believe any of this

For TV, we know the premise

Wikipedia rarely says how a show ends: only for of shows does the summary reach the finale. The score mostly measures the experience a show promises (its characters, its world, its tone), not how it wraps up. Even so, the decline holds when we drop the shows for which we only know the premise.

Votes are memory

“Most voted” is measured with today’s IMDb votes. From the ’90s, people still vote for the shows they remember, mostly sitcoms; from today, whatever is trending. That can exaggerate the gap.

No human has checked them

All the scores come from language models. The materials to validate them with human annotators are ready but haven’t been used yet. The models also recognized almost every title.

A summary isn’t the show

Feel good depends on the music, the faces, the pacing… Here it’s judged from a text. And our definition (peace, love and hope) is just one of several possible.

Lots of comparisons

With so many subgroups, some difference will turn out significant by chance. The results by type of show are exploratory.
